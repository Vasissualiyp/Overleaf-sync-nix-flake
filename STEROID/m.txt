Abstract.
We study the inverse dimensional reduction problem arising in Buckingham Pi
analysis: given a sampled point d in dimensionless space Rm and a map A encoding the Buckingham Pi exponents (Ap = d), recover a physical parameter vector p ∈ Rn satisfying the map
and the physical constraints Λp ≤ c. We present STEROID (Simplicial TEssellation Reduction Of
Invariant Domains), which preprocesses the feasible polytope once and answers subsequent queries
in O(1) time. We benchmark STEROID against six alternative methods across a grid of problem
sizes (n, m). For point queries alone, compiled Dykstra is faster at m ≥ 4 with zero preprocessing,
with a crossover at m ≈ 3–4: the two methods scale in opposite directions with m, and no preprocessing amortization justifies STEROID for pure feasibility queries. However, STEROID provides
precomputed smooth ∂p/∂d, exact one-pass uniform sampling of the feasible dimensionless region,
and geometric characterization of the feasible dimensionless region with no per-query analogue.

1. Introduction. Buckingham’s Π-theorem [9] states that any physically meaningful equation involving n dimensional quantities can be expressed in terms of m =
n − k independent dimensionless groups, where k is the rank of the dimensional ma20 trix (i.e. number of dimensions in kernel of the problem). Dimensional analysis has
21 become a core tool in fluid mechanics, astrophysics, materials science, and engineering
22 design [3, 23, 33, 31]: working in dimensionless space reduces experimental parameter
23 sweeps from n-dimensional to m-dimensional.
24
Applications increasingly require the inverse direction: given a query point d
25 sampled in dimensionless space (e.g., a grid point in a design sweep), find one or more
26 physical parameter vectors p that reproduce d under the Buckingham map and satisfy
27 all physical constraints (ranges of validity, material limits, operating bounds). This
28 problem is under-determined—the preimage A−1 d is an affine subspace of dimension
29 n−m—and the constraint set restricts it to a (possibly empty) convex polytope within
30 that affine subspace.
31
To our knowledge, no dedicated open-source solver exists for this problem. Gorard
32 et al. [18] identify the lack of guarantees that dimensionally reduced parameter combi33 nations remain physically valid as an open challenge in nuclear fusion and high-energy
34 astrophysics. Practitioners typically either resort to brute-force rejection sampling
35 (prohibitively slow), or run a linear program (LP) per query without exploiting the
36 repeated structure of the parameter sweep [3, 23].
37
In this paper we benchmark existing methods for solving the inverse dimensional
38 reduction problem and introduce a novel approach, STEROID, as an alternative.
39 The closest algorithmic relative is explicit linear Model Predictive Control (MPC) [4],
40 which precomputes a piecewise-affine (PWA) map from parameter to optimizer by
41 partitioning the parameter space into critical regions via Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT)
42 conditions. The resulting lookup table supports O(log R) query cost (R = number of
43 regions) via binary search trees [40, 16]. The Delaunay Triangulation approach (a big
44 part of STEROID method) was also explored for explicit MPC by [35].
45

47 proof. Sections 5–6 characterize STEROID’s unique geometric capabilities and GPU48 friendly point location. Section 7 describes the experimental setup, Section 8 presents

3.4. ADMM and Kaczmarz.
Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) [8] solves the feasibility
problem via consensus splitting, with similar per-iteration cost to Dykstra but easier
parameter tuning for ill-conditioned constraint matrices. ADMM converges with an
ergodic O(1/k) rate for convex problems [19, 28], i.e. O(1/ε) iterations to drive the
feasibility residual below ε. Randomized Kaczmarz [38] uses random constraint selection and converges exponentially in expectation: E∥zt − z∗ ∥2 ≤ (1 − κ−2 )t ∥z0 − z∗ ∥2 ,
where κ is the scaled condition number of ΛN . A gradient-penalty method adds a
differentiable penalty ρ∥ max(0, (ΛN )z − (c − ΛA† d))∥2 to a quadratic objective and
minimizes via gradient descent, increasing ρ progressively until feasibility is achieved.

3.5. STEROID: Vertex Enumeration + Delaunay Triangulation.
STEROID invests in a one-time preprocessing step: it identifies the feasible region,
projects it into dimensionless space, triangulates it, and precomputes an affine map
for each simplex. Subsequent queries reduce to locating the containing simplex and
evaluating a single matrix-vector product.
We now walk through the construction step by step.

3.5.2. Projection into dimensionless space.
Each vertex wi ∈ Rn is mapped to dimensionless space via vi = Awi ∈ Rm . The
projected vertices {v1 , . . . , vV } span the feasible dimensionless region D∗ .
Degenerate projections. Multiple physical vertices may project to the same
(or nearly the same) dimensionless point when the polytope P has edges nearly aligned
with ker(A). In this case the Delaunay triangulation produces near-degenerate simplices (det Pi ≈ 0), and the deprojection matrix Ei = Pi′ Pi−1 becomes ill-conditioned.
A natural remedy, left for future implementation, is to merge projected vertices within
a relative tolerance εmerge before triangulation, retaining among merged candidates
the parent vertex farthest from all constraint boundaries (maximizing slack).

3.5.6. Query evaluation. At query time, STEROID performs two operations:

Remark 3.3. The deprojection map d 7→ p is piecewise affine and generally discontinuous across simplex boundaries: adjacent simplices Ti and Tj sharing a facet
may have different parent vertices, producing different p values at the shared boundary. Feasibility is preserved (both outputs lie in P and satisfy Ap = d), but the map
is multi-valued at boundaries. The C k spline interpolation of Section 5.3 resolves this
by enforcing smoothness across simplex boundaries.

5.3. Smooth d → p manifolds via spline interpolation.
The Delaunay triangulation provides a simplicial mesh on D∗ suitable for C k
spline interpolation [26]. Replacing the piecewise-affine STEROID map with a C k
216 spline yields:
217
• Analytical sensitivity: ∂p/∂d available everywhere in D∗ .
218
• Smooth optimization on the feasible manifold.
219
• Geodesic computation with well-defined curvature.
220 The analytic center [30] achieves C ∞ but requires online optimization per query.
221 STEROID + splines achieves C k with precomputed O(1) evaluation. Constructing
222 splines that are continuous across simplex boundaries (i.e., achieving k ≥ 1) requires
223 choosing the spline coefficients to satisfy inter-element compatibility conditions; this
224 is a standard problem in finite element analysis [26, 11, 15] and is deferred to future
225 work. The interpolation accuracy of a degree-r piecewise-polynomial on a simplicial
226 mesh of diameter h is ∥σ − σ ∗ ∥L2 = O(hr+1 ) [15]: the piecewise-affine STEROID map
227 (r = 1) achieves O(h2 ), and a C k spline of degree k + 1 achieves O(hk+2 ).

cannot be formed across queries. Exact uniform sampling of the feasible space is
similarly beyond LP or Dykstra; Volesti’s MCMC is an approximation with burn-in
cost. At m ≥ 6, STEROID’s preprocessing grows exponentially and Dykstra becomes
the only practical option. When a certifiably exact solution is required, LP via HiGHS

exact in the linear case by Theorem 3.2.
Common misconception. It is tempting to argue STEROID is justified whenever preprocessing can be paid once and many queries follow. This is true only at
m ≤ 3 (where STEROID is faster per query) and Q ≳ 105 . For m ≥ 4 and any realistic query count, Dykstra is faster in total (preprocessing + all queries). The correct
justification for STEROID in those regimes is always one of the unique capabilities in
Table 3, not amortized query speed.

approaches differ in how those regions arise.
In explicit MPC, regions are critical regions defined by active constraint sets
from the parametric KKT conditions. Their boundaries are determined by the constraints and the objective function, and can be irregular in shape, numerous in count,
and expensive to enumerate via multi-parametric programming. In STEROID, regions are Delaunay simplices obtained by triangulating the projected feasible vertices. Simplices are well-shaped by the Delaunay circumsphere criterion, and their
count depends on the number of vertices rather than the number of constraint inequalities. Scibilia et al. [35] independently proposed using Delaunay tessellation for
explicit MPC approximation, noting the same regularity advantage. STEROID can
be viewed as an exact variant of that idea applied to the Buckingham Pi setting, where
the Delaunay regions coincide with KKT critical regions whenever the feasible polytope is simple (each vertex is defined by exactly n tight constraints). For non-simple
polytopes the two decompositions differ: KKT regions may be finer, while Delaunay
simplices cover the same domain with fewer, better-conditioned cells.
Buckingham Pi guarantees m ≪ n, so the effective parameter space is genuinely
low-dimensional even for large n. This keeps the Delaunay triangulation tractable at
dimensions m ≤ 4 where explicit MPC would face the curse of dimensionality in KKT
enumeration.
10.3. Limitations and future work. STEROID is infeasible at m ≥ 6 due to
exponential growth in simplex count. Generalized barycentric coordinates [20] offer
a meshfree alternative: given vertices {(vi , wi )}, solve a small LP for barycentric
weights at each query and return a convex combination of preimages. This eliminates
Delaunay entirely while preserving feasibility guarantees. We plan to benchmark this
approach in future work.
The GPU spatial hashing analysis in Section 6 is currently theoretical. Empirical
validation on CUDA hardware would confirm the warp-divergence advantage.
STEROID is currently restricted to the linear map Ap = d; extending it to
coordinate-monotonic non-linear maps remains an open problem, because the image
of a polytope under a non-linear map need not lie within the convex hull of the
projected vertices and a correct treatment requires boundary sampling at additional
computational cost. The active constraint sets of each simplex’s parent vertices are
already known from vertex enumeration, so identifying which physical limits can bind
in each region of D∗ (a phase diagram of binding constraints) is a natural by-product
of the triangulation, but a rigorous phase analysis is left to future work.
The current implementation assumes that the feasible set {p : Λp ≤ c} is a single
convex polytope. Physical problems with disjoint or non-convex feasible regions—
arising, for example, from phase coexistence or multi-modal constraints— require
extensions. A union-of-polytopes representation can handle such cases by running
STEROID independently on each convex component and dispatching at query time
based on the simplex lookup; the main cost is that vertex enumeration must be
repeated per component.
For problems with very large constraint count K, vertex enumeration via PPL
can become the bottleneck. Inexact enumeration strategies—such as pruning lowweight vertices or using randomized double description—can reduce the vertex set at
the cost of missing thin feasible slivers. For applications where approximate coverage
is acceptable, this offers a practical escape from exponential scaling in K.
Finally, for streaming query settings where the dimensionless point d arrives
incrementally, the full triangulation need not be computed upfront. Lazy triangulation
